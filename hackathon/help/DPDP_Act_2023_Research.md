## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)** is India's first comprehensive data protection legislation, enacted by Parliament on 11 August 2023 (Act No. 22 of 2023) and notified by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) on 13 November 2025 alongside the **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025**.

The Act applies to **all businesses processing digital personal data**, regardless of size — with no blanket exemption for MSMEs/small enterprises. Penalties range up to **₹250 crore** for security safeguard failures and **₹200 crore** for breach non-notification, making compliance an existential priority even for the smallest digital businesses.

The framework follows the **SARAL design** — Simple, Accessible, Rational, Actionable — using plain language and illustrations to support ease of understanding and compliance.

### Key Principles of the Act (SARAL Framework)

1. **Consent & Transparency** — Processing only with free, specific, informed, unconditional, unambiguous consent
2. **Purpose Limitation** — Data collected for specified purpose only
3. **Data Minimisation** — Limited to what is necessary
4. **Accuracy** — Personal data must be accurate and up to date
5. **Storage Limitation** — Erased when purpose served or consent withdrawn (unless law requires retention)
6. **Security Safeguards** — Reasonable technical and organisational measures
7. **Accountability** — Data Fiduciary is responsible even when using Data Processors

## 3. PHASED IMPLEMENTATION SCHEDULE (RULES, 2025)

The DPDP Rules, 2025 notified on 13 November 2025 provide an **18-month phased compliance timeline**:

### Phase 1 — Immediate (Gazette Publication: 13 Nov 2025)
- Section 1(2): Commencement provisions
- Section 2: Definitions
- Sections 18–26: Data Protection Board establishment
- Sections 35, 38–44: Protections, directions, rule-making, amendments

### Phase 2 — Within 1 Year (~November 2026)
- Section 6(9): Consent Manager registration requirements
- Section 27(1)(d): Board powers regarding Consent Manager registration breaches
- **Consent Managers must be Indian companies**

### Phase 3 — Within 18 Months (~May 2027)
- Sections 3–5: Lawful processing grounds & Notice
- Section 6(1–8, 10): Consent framework
- Sections 7–10: Legitimate uses & Significant Data Fiduciary
- Sections 11–17: Data Principal rights & duties
- Sections 27–34: Board powers & procedures
- Sections 36–37: Information gathering & government directions
- Section 44(2): Difficulty removal

## 4. KEY DEFINITIONS (SECTION 2)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Personal Data** | Any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to such data |
| **Data Principal** | The individual to whom the personal data relates |
| **Data Fiduciary** | Entity that determines the purpose and means of processing personal data |
| **Data Processor** | Entity that processes personal data on behalf of a Data Fiduciary |
| **Personal Data Breach** | Any unauthorised processing or accidental disclosure, acquisition, sharing, use, alteration, destruction or loss of access to personal data that compromises confidentiality, integrity or availability |
| **Processing** | Wholly or partly automated operation on digital personal data (collection, recording, organisation, storage, retrieval, use, sharing, disclosure, erasure, destruction) |
| **Significant Data Fiduciary** | Any Data Fiduciary or class notified by Central Government under Section 10 |
| **Consent Manager** | Entity registered with the Board to help individuals manage consent |
| **Specified Purpose** | Purpose mentioned in the notice given by Data Fiduciary to Data Principal |

## 5. SCOPE & APPLICABILITY (SECTION 3)

The Act applies to:
- Processing of digital personal data within India
- Processing outside India if it is connected to profiling or offering goods/services to Data Principals in India

**No blanket exemption for SMEs** — All digital Data Fiduciaries are liable regardless of size.

---

## 6. GROUNDS FOR PROCESSING (SECTION 4)

Personal data may be processed only for a **lawful purpose**:
- (a) For which the Data Principal has given consent; OR
- (b) For certain legitimate uses under Section 7

**Lawful purpose** = Any purpose not expressly forbidden by law.

---

## 7. NOTICE REQUIREMENTS (SECTION 5)

### Section 5(1) — Notice before/during consent request
Every request for consent must be accompanied or preceded by a notice informing the Data Principal of:
- (i) The personal data and purpose for which it is proposed to be processed
- (ii) The manner in which she may exercise her rights under Section 6(4) and Section 13
- (iii) The manner in which she may make a complaint to the Board

### Section 5(2) — Legacy data notice
For data collected before Act commencement, the Data Fiduciary must give notice as soon as reasonably practicable.

### Section 5(3) — Language
Notice must be available in **English or any language specified in the Eighth Schedule** to the Constitution (22 languages including Hindi, Kannada, Tamil, etc.).

### Section 5 — Rules Requirements (DPDP Rules 2025, Rule 3)
- Notice must be presented independently of other information
- Clear and plain language
- Itemised description of personal data collected
- Specified purpose(s) of processing
- Communication link for withdrawing consent, exercising rights, making complaints

---

## 8. CONSENT FRAMEWORK (SECTION 6)

### Section 6(1) — Valid Consent Standard
Consent must be:
- **Free** — Without coercion
- **Specific** — For a defined purpose
- **Informed** — Data Principal understands what they're agreeing to
- **Unconditional** — Not bundled with unrelated terms
- **Unambiguous** — Clear affirmative action
- **Clear affirmative action** — Positive opt-in, not pre-ticked boxes
- **Limited to necessary personal data** — Data minimisation

### Section 6(4) — Right to Withdraw Consent
- Data Principal may withdraw consent **at any time**
- Withdrawal must be **as easy as giving consent**
- Consequences borne by Data Principal (e.g., loss of service access)

### Section 6(6) — Post-Withdrawal Obligation
Data Fiduciary must cease processing (and cause Data Processors to cease) within a reasonable time, unless processing is required by law.

### Section 6(7–10) — Consent Manager
- Consent may be given/managed/withdrawn through a registered Consent Manager
- Consent Manager accountable to Data Principal
- Every Consent Manager registered with the Board
- Data Fiduciary must prove consent was validly obtained

### Section 6(9) — Effective Date (Phase 2)
Comes into force **1 year after Gazette publication** (~November 2026).

---

## 9. LEGITIMATE USES (SECTION 7)

Personal data may be processed without consent for:
- Voluntarily provided data where Data Principal has not indicated non-consent
- State functions, sovereignty, security of India
- Legal obligations to disclose information
- Compliance with court judgments/decrees
- Medical emergencies (threat to life/health)
- Epidemic/disease outbreak response
- Disaster response / public order breakdown
- Employment-related purposes (preventing corporate espionage, trade secrets, employee benefits)

---

## 10. DATA FIDUCIARY OBLIGATIONS (SECTION 8)

### Section 8(1) — Primary Responsibility
Data Fiduciary is responsible for compliance **irrespective of agreements or Data Principal duties**, including processing by Data Processors.

### Section 8(2) — Data Processor Engagement
Only under a valid contract.

### Section 8(3) — Data Completeness & Accuracy
Must ensure completeness, accuracy, consistency if data used for decisions affecting Data Principal or disclosed to another fiduciary.

### Section 8(4) — Technical & Organisational Measures
Must implement appropriate measures for effective observance of the Act.

### Section 8(5) — Security Safeguards
**Must protect personal data** including processing by Data Processors, through reasonable security safeguards (encryption, obfuscation, masking, virtual tokens, access control, logging, monitoring).

### Section 8(6) — Breach Notification
**Must give intimation of personal data breach** to:
- The Data Protection Board of India
- Each affected Data Principal
In such form and manner as prescribed.

### Section 8(7) — Erasure Obligation
**Unless retention is necessary for compliance with any law**, Data Fiduciary shall:
- (a) Erase personal data upon consent withdrawal or when purpose no longer served
- (b) Cause Data Processors to erase data

**CRITICAL STATUTORY CONFLICT:** Section 8(7) erasure vs. CGST Act Section 36 (6-year invoice retention) — covered in Section 19 below.

## 11. CHILDREN'S DATA (SECTION 9)

- Verifiable consent required before processing children's personal data
- Limited exemptions for healthcare, education, real-time safety
- No tracking, behavioural monitoring, or targeted advertising detrimental to children

---

## 12. SIGNIFICANT DATA FIDUCIARY (SECTION 10)

- Additional obligations including independent audits, impact assessments
- Government-specified restrictions on certain data categories, including localisation

---

## 13. DATA PRINCIPAL RIGHTS — SECTIONS 11–14

### Section 11 — Right to Access
Upon request, Data Principal may obtain:
- (a) Summary of personal data being processed & processing activities
- (b) Identities of all other Data Fiduciaries/Processors data shared with
- (c) Any other prescribed information

### Section 12 — Right to Correction & Erasure
- Right to correct inaccurate data, complete incomplete data, update data
- Right to erasure (subject to retention requirements under law)
- Data Fiduciary must correct/complete/update upon verified request

### Section 13 — Right to Grievance Redressal
- Readily available grievance redressal mechanism
- Data Fiduciary/Consent Manager must respond within prescribed period
- Data Principal must exhaust this before approaching Board

### Section 14 — Right to Nominate
- Right to nominate another person to exercise rights in event of death/incapacity

---

## 14. DATA PRINCIPAL DUTIES (SECTION 15)

- Comply with all applicable laws
- Not impersonate another person
- Not suppress material information
- Not register false/frivolous grievances
- Furnish only verifiably authentic information

---

## 15. DATA PROTECTION BOARD OF INDIA (SECTIONS 18–26)

### Section 18 — Establishment
Independent body, digital by design (complaints, allocation, hearing, decisions digital).

### Section 27 — Powers & Functions
- Direct urgent remedial measures on breach intimation (Section 8(6))
- Inquire into breaches & impose penalties
- Handle Data Principal complaints
- Issue directions binding on persons

### Section 28 — Digital Functioning
Digital office by design; techno-legal measures as prescribed.

## 16. PENALTIES & ADJUDICATION (SECTION 33 & SCHEDULE)

### Section 33(1) — Monetary Penalties
Board may impose monetary penalty after inquiry and opportunity to be heard.

### Section 33(2) — Factors Considered
Nature, gravity, duration of breach; type of data affected; repetitiveness; gain/loss; mitigation efforts; proportionality; impact on person.

### THE SCHEDULE — Penalty Matrix

| Sl. No. | Breach of Provision | Maximum Penalty |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Section 8(5) — Reasonable security safeguards to prevent breach | **₹250 Crore** |
| 2 | Section 8(6) — Notify Board/Data Principals of breach | **₹200 Crore** |
| 3 | Section 9 — Obligations relating to children | **₹200 Crore** |
| 4 | Section 10 — Significant Data Fiduciary obligations | **₹150 Crore** |
| 5 | Section 15 — Data Principal duties | **₹10,000** |
| 6 | Section 32 — Voluntary undertaking terms | Applicable extent |
| 7 | Any other provision | **₹50 Crore** |

**Key Takeaway:** The two highest penalties (₹250 Cr for security, ₹200 Cr for breach non-notification) make compliance an existential priority for SMEs.

## 17. DPDP RULES, 2025 — KEY PROVISIONS

### Rule 3 — Notice by Data Fiduciary to Data Principal
- Presented independently of other information
- Clear and plain language with itemised data description & purpose
- Communication link for withdrawing consent, exercising rights, making complaints

### Rule 4 — Consent Manager Registration
- Must be Indian company
- Registered with Board under technical/operational/financial conditions

### Rule 6 — Reasonable Security Safeguards
- Encryption, obfuscation, masking, virtual tokens
- Access control
- Logging, monitoring, detection
- Data backups
- Breach detection & remediation

### Rule 7 — Intimation of Personal Data Breach
**(1) To Data Principals:** Without delay, concise/clear/plain description including:
- (a) Nature, extent, timing of breach
- (b) Consequences relevant to them
- (c) Measures implemented to mitigate risk
- (d) Safety measures they may take
- (e) Business contact information

**(2) To Board:** Without delay (description); within 72 hours (updated detailed info, circumstances, mitigation measures, findings, remedial measures, report)

### Rule 13 — Significant Data Fiduciary Obligations
- Annual Data Protection Impact Assessment & audit
- Report to Board

## 18. COMPLIANCE IMPLICATIONS FOR INDIAN SMEs

### Why SMEs Cannot Ignore DPDP Act

1. **No size exemption** — All digital Data Fiduciaries liable regardless of turnover/employee count
2. **Existence-threatening penalties** — ₹250 Cr (security) / ₹200 Cr (breach non-notification) can bankrupt an SME
3. **Informal data practices are non-compliant** — WhatsApp chats, unencrypted Excel sheets, shared billing software all constitute "processing" under Section 2(u)
4. **Consent is mandatory** — Cannot collect phone numbers, Aadhaar, medical data without Section 6-compliant consent
5. **Breach reporting is mandatory** — Lost phone, leaked link, or accidental email triggers Section 8(6) reporting to DPBI and affected customers
6. **Erasure rights create operational burden** — Right to erasure (Section 12) must be balanced with tax retention laws (CGST Section 36)

### SME-Specific Compliance Checklist

| Requirement | DPDP Section | SME Action |
|---|---|---|
| Appoint Data Protection Officer/Contact | Rule 7(1)(e) | Display contact on premises/website |
| Provide Section 5 Notice | Section 5 | Plain-language notice before data collection |
| Obtain Section 6 Consent | Section 6 | Affirmative opt-in, easy withdrawal |
| Implement Security Safeguards | Section 8(5) | Encryption, access control, backups |
| Breach Reporting | Section 8(6) + Rule 7 | Notify Board + affected customers without delay |
| Erasure on Request | Section 12(3) | Erase unless law requires retention |
| Grievance Redressal | Section 13 | Respond within prescribed period |
| Maintain RoPA | Section 8(4) | Record of processing activities |

## 19. STATUTORY CONFLICT: DPDP ERASURE vs. CGST/INCOME TAX RETENTION

This is the **critical domain masterstroke** for the LS-04 solution.

### The Conflict

| Provision | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **DPDP Act Section 8(7)** | Erase personal data when consent withdrawn or purpose no longer served (unless retention required by law) |
| **CGST Act Section 36** | Retain books of account/invoices for 72 months (6 years) |
| **Income Tax Act** | Retain records for 6–8 years |

### Resolution — Selective Pseudonymisation

| Invoice Age | Action | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| < 6 years | **STATUTORY RETENTION LOCK** — Financial data preserved; marketing tags/contact data erased | CGST Act Section 36 read with DPDP Act Section 8(7) |
| ≥ 6 years | **APPROVED_FULL_ERASURE** — Complete redaction permitted | DPDP Act Sections 12(2) & 8(7) |

**Practical Implementation:**
- Erasure engine checks invoice date against current date
- If ≥ 6 years: Full erasure approved, SHA-256 certificate issued
- If < 6 years: Retention lock applied; only non-financial personal data (phone, address, marketing tags) redacted; financial records preserved for tax audit

## 22. CONCLUSION

The DPDP Act, 2023 creates a comprehensive, enforceable data protection regime applicable to **all Indian businesses processing digital personal data**, with no SME exemption. The phased implementation through November 2025–May 2027 gives SMEs a compliance runway, but penalties of up to ₹250 Crore make proactive compliance essential.

DPDP_Secure addresses this market gap by providing an **offline-first, zero-cloud-leakage compliance engine** specifically designed for informal SME systems (WhatsApp chats, Excel sheets, billing software) — the exact environment described in LS-04.
